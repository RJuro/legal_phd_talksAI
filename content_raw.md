### Analysis of Student Papers & How Your Talk Can Address Their Themes

I have synthesized the key themes from the OCR'd papers. For each theme, I'll outline what they are saying and how you can connect your talk directly to it.

#### Theme 1: The Duality of AI: The "Helpful Assistant" vs. The "Confident Liar"

*   **What They're Saying:** This is the most dominant theme. Nearly every student sees AI as a powerful tool for efficiency: summarizing texts, finding case law, translating, checking grammar, and brainstorming (they name tools like KAILA, Pandektes, Copilot). Simultaneously, they are acutely aware of the risks of **hallucinations**, **false citations**, and the "black box" nature of the technology. They feel the tension between utility and unreliability on a personal, practical level.

*   **How to Align Your Talk:**
    1.  **Name the Tension:** Give their intuition a formal name. Call it the **"Capability-Reliability Gap."** Explain that modern AI has crossed a critical threshold in *capability* (it can perform tasks that look like legal work) but lags dangerously in *reliability* (it cannot be trusted to do so accurately or honestly).
    2.  **Provide the "Why":** As a technologist, you can explain *why* this gap exists. Talk simply about how Large Language Models (LLMs) are probabilistic token predictors, not logical reasoning engines. They are designed to generate plausible text, not to verify facts. This technical insight moves them from "it makes mistakes" to "I understand its fundamental architectural limitation."
    3.  **Introduce the Economic Incentive:** As an economist, explain that the market is incentivized to push *capability* first, because it's flashy and demonstrates value, while *reliability* is a costly, slow, and less visible feature. This explains *why* they are being handed these powerful but flawed tools and puts the responsibility on organizations to manage the risk.

*   **Slide/Talking Point Idea:** A slide with two curves. One, a steep upward curve labeled "Capability (What it can do)." The other, a much flatter, slower curve labeled "Reliability (What you can trust)." The large space between the curves is labeled **"The Liability Zone: Your New Workplace."**

#### Theme 2: Fear of Deskilling and Loss of Critical Thought

*   **What They're Saying:** Several students express a sophisticated concern that over-reliance on AI could atrophy their own cognitive skills. One paper mentions a study showing users with AI access underperformed and had lower cognitive engagement. They worry about becoming passive consumers of AI output rather than active, critical thinkers. They understand the need for *kildekritik* (source criticism) is now paramount.

*   **How to Align Your Talk:**
    1.  **Validate This Concern:** Acknowledge that this is a known phenomenon in automation, sometimes called "automation complacency."
    2.  **Reframe the Skill:** Argue that the core legal skill isn't changing, but the *workflow* is. The new critical skill is not just legal analysis, but **AI output validation**. Frame it as an active, high-value task.
    3.  **Provide a Framework:** Offer them a practical model. Suggest they treat AI not as a colleague, but as a **"Zero-Trust, Infinitely Fast, Occasionally Brilliant Intern."** You would never copy-paste an intern's work into a legal filing without checking every single source and assumption. The same discipline applies here, but at machine speed. This gives them a powerful, memorable metaphor for the new workflow.

#### Theme 3: Systemic Bias and Fairness

*   **What They're Saying:** They are aware of algorithmic bias, citing the COMPAS algorithm as a classic example. They understand that AI trained on historical data can perpetuate and even amplify existing societal biases, undermining the very purpose of a just legal system. One student is even researching algorithmic bias in sentencing.

*   **How to Align Your Talk:**
    1.  **Go Beyond the Obvious:** They already know about biased data. Your contribution is to talk about the *measurement* of fairness.
    2.  **Introduce Benchmarking as Governance:** This is a key area for your expertise. Explain that in the world of AI, "fairness" is not a vague ideal; it's a set of quantifiable metrics (e.g., demographic parity, equality of opportunity). The choice of which metric to optimize for is a *political and ethical* decision, not a technical one.
    3.  **Provocative Question:** Ask them: "Who should be responsible for defining and auditing the fairness benchmarks for legal AI in Denmark? The vendor? The Ministry of Justice? An independent body?" This turns a technical topic into a live question of governance and legal oversight, right in their wheelhouse.

#### Theme 4: AI in the Institution: From Research Tool to Actor in the System

*   **What They're Saying:** They discuss AI's role not just in their PhDs, but in the legal system itself: predictive policing, automating case management to reduce backlogs (*sagsbehandlingstid*), and as decision-support tools. They rightly question whether this removes the essential "human element" (*menneskeligt element*) and discretion.

*   **How to Align Your Talk:**
    1.  **Introduce "Law as Code":** This is your most powerful and constructive contribution. Many of them see AI as an opaque text-generator. Introduce the more structured, transparent concept of **"Rules as Code"** or **"Law as Code."**
    2.  **Explain the Concept:** Describe how governments (like in New Zealand and France) are already experimenting with drafting legislation and regulations in both human language and machine-readable code. This code is transparent, auditable, and its logic can be tested.
    3.  **Connect it to Their Concerns:** Frame "Law as Code" as a potential antidote to the "black box" problem. Instead of a mysterious AI making a decision, you have a transparent, coded rule being applied. This shifts the debate from "can we trust the AI?" to "did we write the rule correctly?"—a much more familiar legal question.

1.  **Introduction: The Outsider's Field Report.** (5 mins)
    *   Acknowledge their work. State your perspective (economist, technologist).
    *   Frame the talk around providing systems-level frameworks to understand the changes they're grappling with.

2.  **The Core Tension: Navigating the Capability-Reliability Gap.** (10 mins)
    *   Define the gap. Explain the technical and economic reasons behind it.
    *   Use the "Liability Zone" visual. This directly addresses their primary concern with hallucinations.

3.  **The New Workflow: The Zero-Trust Intern.** (10 mins)
    *   Address the fear of deskilling.
    *   Reframe critical thinking as an active validation workflow.
    *   Provide this memorable, practical metaphor for working with AI.

4.  **From Vague Fears to Hard Metrics: Benchmarking as Governance.** (15 mins)
    *   Explain what benchmarks are.
    *   Show how they can be used to hide bias or, if designed well, to enforce fairness.
    *   Distinguish between "Performance Benchmarks" (Did it pass the bar exam?) and "Liability Benchmarks" (Can I safely file its output?).
    *   Pose the governance question: Who writes the benchmarks for law?

5.  **A Constructive Future: "Unit Testing" Law Before It Happens.** (15 mins)
    *   Introduce "Law as Code" as a more transparent alternative to black-box AI.
    *   Explain the concept with real-world examples (NZ, France).
    *   Introduce the powerful idea of simulating a law's impact on synthetic populations to find fairness gaps and loopholes *before* deployment.

6.  **Conclusion: Your Role as Architects of the New System.** (5 mins)
    *   Summarize: The challenge is not the tech itself, but the lack of robust systems for **Validation, Benchmarking, and Simulation.**
    *   End with an empowering message: As legal scholars, they are not just users or victims of this technology. They are uniquely positioned to be the *architects* of the legal and ethical frameworks needed to govern it.

By structuring your talk this way, you meet them exactly where they are, validate their concerns, provide them with new and powerful conceptual tools from your field, and leave them with a constructive and empowering vision for the future of their own research and practice.
